# GAIA — The Ecology Agent
## A scientific and systems specification for living plant worlds and generative cuboid-voxel growth

**Document type:** Expanded design paper and implementation proposal  
**Supersedes:** No existing authority by itself; expands the design record `GAIA.md`

## Abstract

Gaia is an autonomous ecology agent for an emergent living world. It uses the project's declared authority machinery, conservation ledger, deterministic process roster, and evidence records, but removes the human-approval gate for its own ecological decisions. That exception is deliberately narrow: it does not remove conservation, atomicity, bounded population, deterministic scheduling, replay evidence, or the player's ability to make binding physical interventions.

This paper connects that architecture to plant biology and turns it into an implementation-oriented model of plant life. It covers modular plant development, growth and resource limitation, water and nutrient relations, environmental sensing and tropisms, reproduction and dormancy, ecological interactions, soil feedback, biomechanical constraints, and the difference between biological response and Gaia's game-level disposition system. It also proposes a generative architecture in which plant form is produced by a development graph and constrained by **cuboid voxel shaper profiles**. A shaper profile controls the spatial envelope and distribution of stems, crowns, roots, leaves, fronds, or vines; it does not substitute for biology, create biomass, or override conserved transfers.

The scientific framework draws on functional–structural plant modelling (FSPM), plant architecture research, source–sink physiology, plant morphogenesis, phyllotaxis, tropisms, seed ecology, and plant–soil interactions. FSPM is particularly relevant because it couples three-dimensional structure to plant function and explicitly treats geometry, topology, environment, and function as complementary parts of one model. Recent guidance also identifies voxels, crown envelopes, L-systems, and graph grammars as viable structural and developmental representations when chosen to fit the model's intended scale and computational budget ([Vos et al. and subsequent FSPM literature](https://academic.oup.com/insilicoplants/article/8/2/diag023/8770950); [Prusinkiewicz et al.](https://algorithmicbotany.org/papers/modeling-plant-development-with-l-systems.html)).

The result is a design in which Gaia can have declared preferences and act autonomously without becoming a magical source of matter, an unbounded simulator, or an authority that can revoke physical changes made by the player.

## 1. Purpose, scope, and non-goals

### 1.1 Purpose

The purpose of Gaia is to make ecology an active participant in the world rather than a passive collection of decorative rules. Plants should grow, compete, reproduce, respond to conditions, die, spread, and alter their local biological environment through admitted processes. Some species may be benign; others may be invasive, defensive, mobile, or dangerous. Their behaviour is generated by a combination of species traits, current state, environmental constraints, and Gaia's declared disposition toward species and claims.

The desired outcome is not a perfectly faithful digital twin of a real ecosystem. It is a **scientifically constrained simulation** whose abstractions are explicit, testable, and internally consistent. Biological realism is applied where it strengthens emergence, explainability, and play. Deliberate game rules remain permitted, provided they are identified as such and do not masquerade as empirical botany.

### 1.2 Non-goals

Gaia is not intended to:

- claim that actual plants possess human-like consciousness, language, or moral reasoning;
- simulate every biochemical reaction, cell, microorganism, or molecule;
- generate unrestricted terrain edits outside the declared terrain-process roster;
- mint matter, bypass ledger accounting, or evade atomic commits;
- make a plant's voxel count a substitute for its physical mass;
- treat a procedural shape template as a complete model of plant physiology;
- allow hidden disposition numbers to be presented as measured facts;
- require a per-individual decision log for every population member on every cadence tick.

### 1.3 Scientific framing of “agency”

In this specification, **plant agency** is a game-architecture term for autonomous state-driven behaviour: an organism can respond to local cues, grow, select among feasible developmental actions, and affect its surroundings. It is not a claim that plants have animal-like nervous systems or human-like intentions. Plants are capable of complex sensing and signalling, including light responses, gravity responses, touch responses, hormone-mediated regulation, electrical signals, and chemical exchanges. These phenomena are sufficient to ground a rich simulation without attributing human psychology to a plant. For example, phototropism involves directional light detection and differential growth regulation, with auxin transport participating in the response ([phototropism review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3963583/)).

Gaia's preferences, hostility, redemption, and appreciation are therefore **fictional control variables** layered over biologically feasible plant behaviour. The simulation should keep them distinct from measurable physiology.

## 2. Existing GAIA rules preserved by this expansion

This section records the core mechanics in the source design. It is not a replacement for the source's numbered decisions.

### 2.1 Autonomous ecological action with conserved outcomes

**Existing GAIA rule (`0128`, `0140`):** Gaia can initiate life processes without human approval. Examples include animal birth and excretion, plant life cycles, vine growth, predatory pathfinding by deadly vines, and rapid bug reproduction. Every action still produces a conserved delta and a full evidence record. Mass-bearing actions remain atomic under `0105`.

**Interpretation:** The absence of a human approval gate does not mean the absence of governance. Gaia can initiate an admitted process; the process must still satisfy its preconditions, account for matter, produce an explicit delta, and commit atomically or refuse.

### 2.2 Requests are advisory; physical interventions are binding

**Existing GAIA rule (`0131`):** A refusal to a Gaia request is advisory. Gaia may act on a later cadence, with that later action made visible to the player. A physical intervention by the player is binding because it changes matter rather than merely expressing permission. Gaia may resent, avoid, or route around a fence, but it cannot undo the fence.

**Implementation consequence:** Do not model player consent as a persistent veto that Gaia can silently bypass, and do not model Gaia's preference as authority to reverse a physical intervention. A refusal can alter Gaia's later choices; it cannot erase matter-level recourse.

### 2.3 Disposition vector

**Existing GAIA rule (`0130`, `0132`):** Gaia tracks three coupled but independent axes per species and per claim, not normally per individual:

| Axis | Meaning in GAIA | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Hostility** | How much Gaia acts against a species or claim | Not a universal measure of biological aggression |
| **Redemption** | How far the species/claim can be brought back | Can be high even when hostility is high |
| **Appreciation** | How much Gaia favours a species or claim | Preference does not create resources or grant impossible capabilities |

Redemption can be earned through declared visible player acts, passive declared time, and Gaia's own initiative when appreciation crosses a declared threshold. Gaia does not arbitrarily declare forgiveness; state changes must follow the declared function.

### 2.4 Hidden disposition and observable outcomes

**Existing GAIA rule (`0147`):** Disposition is hidden; behaviour is the tell. Players infer Gaia's state from growth, reach, pathfinding, herd response, soil changes, and other observable outcomes. A UI must not imply an unmeasured disposition. A technology branch may eventually unlock reading disposition, requesting rather than merely acting, and opening redemption paths.

**Design implication:** The simulation may maintain numerical internal variables while the ordinary player interface exposes only outcomes. Later technology unlocks can change the permitted observation model; they should not retroactively make earlier unmeasured displays truthful.

### 2.5 Hostility boundaries and mass thresholds

**Existing GAIA rule (`0144`, `0146`):** Vines cannot bite. Deadly plants may act aggressively against civilians or damage the environment when strong enough. Strength is determined by comparing the plant's conserved mass with a declared fraction of the target civilian's conserved mass. Each species has a hard mass ceiling above which it cannot grow.

**Design implication:** The threat check must use ledger-backed mass, not a renderer's apparent size, number of occupied voxels, an arbitrary combat level, or an unverified AI judgement. A species ceiling creates a hard maximum, not a soft preference.

### 2.6 Gaia acts through living things; terrain uses declared processes

**Existing GAIA rule (`0148`):** Gaia acts on living things, not directly on substrate. Terrain reshaping must use the existing declared process roster. The nutrient cycle can be driven through organism → excretion → soil account → declared fertility → plant growth, with growth drawing on the exact admitted transfer (`0145`). Gaia can drive a declared process but cannot bypass it.

**Scientific extension discussed below:** A fuller plant ledger should also represent atmospheric carbon dioxide and water inputs, plant litter and decomposition, and mineral transfers. This expands the roster of explicit accounts and processes; it must not be implemented as an unreviewed bypass of the existing source-account rules.

### 2.7 Mobile flora, deterministic paths, and authority

**Existing GAIA rules (`0141`–`0142`, `0153`):** A vine is an entity on the same governed actor pipeline as a citizen. It has a position, target selection, movement delta, and evidence record. It is not merely a voxel property. Pathfinding uses stable job keys and bounded declared search with a fixed tie-break order; a greedy local walk is permitted only as a declared deterministic alternative. A vine is a target rather than an authority holder: it can be destroyed without approval and cannot initiate governed work, claim ground, or open a tender.

**Implementation consequence:** Voxel geometry can render and constrain a vine's body, but it must not replace the vine's actor identity, movement state, target-selection process, or authority limits.

### 2.8 Population, cadence, and logs

**Existing GAIA rules (`0121`, `0143`, `0150`, `0155`–`0158`):** Populations have declared carrying capacity per region and a global entity ceiling. Births that exceed admitted limits are refused with typed refusals, not silently dropped. Distant populations may use level of detail (LOD). Gaia runs on its own declared cadence, separate from the general ecology tick; cadence may adapt to authoritative state through a declared deterministic function. The colony has one aggregate disposition entry plus one per claim. The log records disposition deltas per species × region × cadence rather than individual decision records.

**Implementation consequence:** LOD must retain enough aggregate state to preserve matter, age/stage distributions, reproductive potential, and later expansion decisions. A population cap must not be evaded by replacing many entities with an aggregate that can later unfold into unaccounted entities.

### 2.9 Sustainable practice and mod extensibility

**Existing GAIA rules (`0154`, `0157`, `0149`, `0151`, `0159`):** A declared practice tag indicates intent, but a measured outcome confirms it. Gaia responds to measured results; the tag determines which outcomes count. A privileged-tier mod may rewrite Gaia's preference and hostility rules, but conservation, authority, and replay rules remain canonical. Every Gaia decision record names the active ruleset identity, although the source design deliberately does not identity-bind that ruleset; behaviour remains explainable across mod changes but is not guaranteed to reproduce identically after a ruleset change.

The practice vocabulary and the technology node set remain open items in the source record. Proposed extensions in this paper are candidates, not settled registry entries.

## 3. Scientific foundations for plant life

### 3.1 Plant form is modular and developmentally constrained

Many plants construct their bodies by adding modules over time. Shoots, leaves, branches, roots, flowers, fruits, and storage organs originate through developmentally regulated growth zones such as meristems. A plant's final architecture is not just a static species mesh: it reflects its inherited developmental programme interacting with light, water, nutrients, temperature, mechanical stress, damage, and competition. Reviews of plant architecture describe how genetic regulation and environmental conditions jointly shape branching, leaf arrangement, organ initiation, and the continuation or termination of growth ([Plant architecture](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1084230/); [Evolution and ecology of plant architecture](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5710528/)).

This modularity is well suited to an agent-driven world. A plant need not be regenerated as one enormous mesh after every change. It can add a branch, extend a root, produce a leaf, seal a damaged growth point, set a flower, or shed an organ as discrete developmental events.

**Proposed extension:** Represent the plant as an organ graph. Nodes are organs or growth points; edges encode parentage, vascular or structural connection, and developmental order. Each node carries its organ type, age, orientation, mass allocation, physiological state, and local shaper profile. The graph records topology; the voxel shaper records an allowable or preferred geometric envelope.

### 3.2 Growth has several distinct meanings

The word *growth* can refer to different processes at different scales:

- **Developmental growth:** meristems initiate cells and organs.
- **Expansion:** existing tissues elongate or widen.
- **Biomass accumulation:** organic and mineral matter is incorporated into new tissue.
- **Population growth:** more individuals establish through seed, spore, fragmentation, or clonal spread.
- **Ecosystem production:** biomass accumulates or is lost over a region and interval.

These concepts are related but not interchangeable. A plant can expand a water-rich leaf without a proportional increase in dry biomass, or have stored carbon while growth is constrained by water, temperature, nutrients, or the inability of a sink organ to expand. A comprehensive review stresses that growth metrics and limiting mechanisms vary with biological scale and context ([Hilty, “Plant growth: the What, the How, and the Why”](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.17610)).

**Simulation rule:** Do not use a single “growth points” variable as if it were simultaneously biomass, tissue expansion, age, reproductive readiness, and ecological fitness. Separate the minimum set needed by the model: age/stage, organ geometry, matter stores, resource status, and reproductive state.

### 3.3 Photosynthesis, respiration, and the mass ledger

Plants use light energy to drive photosynthetic carbon assimilation. In simplified form, atmospheric carbon dioxide and water are converted into reduced carbon compounds, with oxygen released. The resulting carbohydrates can be used for construction, energy metabolism, storage, transport, exudation, reproduction, or defence. Respiration and other losses consume or export part of the assimilated carbon. Plant growth therefore depends on both sources (assimilation, stored reserves, transported resources) and sinks (growing organs and other destinations). Source–sink reviews emphasise that carbon supply alone is not always the sole limit; transport and a growing organ's capacity to use assimilates also matter ([source–sink interactions review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1674205222004348); [modelling carbon sources and sinks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30339280/)).

This matters directly for GAIA's conservation rule. **Light is energy, not matter.** Most of the carbon in plant organic matter comes from CO₂ in the atmosphere, while water contributes hydrogen and oxygen and soil provides essential mineral elements such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, and others. If the only admitted mass source for plant growth is a soil fertility account, the simulation can conserve its own numbers while misrepresenting how real plant biomass is assembled.

**Existing GAIA rule:** plant growth is a declared transfer drawing on the soil account, and no process may bypass the matter ledger.

**Proposed scientific extension:** Extend the declared process roster and source-account schema so growth can debit an explicit combination of:

1. atmospheric CO₂/carbon availability (or a coarse-grained atmospheric carbon account);
2. water availability;
3. mineral nutrient accounts in soil or other admitted reservoirs;
4. parent/seed/propagule reserves, where applicable;
5. stored plant reserves when an organ is constructed from previously accumulated matter.

The corresponding outputs must include any represented oxygen, water, CO₂, exudates, shed organs, and other exported matter. A simple game may use a coarse-grained biomass packet rather than full elemental chemistry, but it must define what that packet means and balance its debits and credits. A higher-fidelity mode can track elemental C, H, O, N, P, K, and other elements separately. The exact chemistry model is a precision/performance choice; an undocumented free-mass shortcut is not.

### 3.4 Resource acquisition and allocation

Plants cannot relocate their entire bodies to escape a bad patch. They alter root architecture, leaf placement, branching, and reproductive investment while drawing resources from a local environment. A useful simplified model distinguishes:

- **Acquisition:** capture of light, uptake of water, and acquisition of mineral nutrients.
- **Transport:** movement of water and solutes through plant tissues.
- **Allocation:** distribution of assimilated matter among roots, stems, leaves, storage organs, flowers, fruits, seeds, repair, and defence.
- **Maintenance and loss:** respiration, tissue death, herbivory, damage, exudation, and failed growth.

Allocation should be trait-driven and stage-sensitive, not a universal constant. Seedlings may invest heavily in establishment; drought-stressed plants may shift allocation; reproductive plants may direct matter to flowers and seeds; damaged plants may repair or redirect growth. Global evidence on root-to-shoot ratios identifies environmental and plant-trait influences on biomass allocation, while also warning that allocation schemes used in models require empirical care ([root:shoot ratio assessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12501925/)).

**Proposed extension:** Give every species a declared allocation policy, such as a target fraction or priority order for root, support, photosynthetic, storage, and reproductive organs. Let environmental stress and stage modify those targets through declared functions. Actual mass allocated is still limited by available accounts and organ-level sink capacity.

### 3.5 Water relations, transpiration, and hydraulic limits

Water is not merely a binary “wet/dry” label. Water availability affects cell expansion, transport, stomatal regulation, photosynthesis, nutrient movement, seed germination, and survival. Water travels through soil, roots, xylem, leaves, and atmosphere along water-potential gradients; transpiration is coupled to gas exchange. Drought can reduce expansion and growth before it causes a matching reduction in photosynthesis, so a plant may have assimilated carbon that it cannot turn into new tissue at the same rate ([plant growth review](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.17610)).

A game-scale implementation need not solve water potential at the xylem-cell scale. It should nevertheless distinguish at least:

- soil water available in the active root zone;
- root-accessible water rather than total regional water;
- water transport capacity or a declared proxy;
- transpiration demand influenced by leaf area and atmospheric conditions;
- stress thresholds for reduced expansion, stomatal closure, dormancy, and death.

**Proposed extension:** Make a plant's effective water supply depend on root distribution and local soil moisture, not simply the moisture value of the voxel containing its stem. Larger leaf area can increase potential water demand; deeper or wider roots can acquire water from more cells but cost matter to construct and maintain.

### 3.6 Mineral nutrition and plant–soil feedback

Plants acquire essential mineral nutrients from the substrate, often in forms made available by weathering, organic matter decomposition, microbial processes, and chemical transformations. Root uptake is selective and constrained by root distribution, soil chemistry, moisture, and competing organisms. Mycorrhizal fungi and bacteria can modify acquisition and nutrient cycling; these relationships are not uniform across all plants or ecosystems. A 2024 review discusses cross-kingdom nutrient exchange across plant–arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungus–bacterium associations ([Nature Reviews Microbiology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41579-024-01073-7)).

GAIA's existing cycle — organism → excretion → soil account → declared fertility → plant growth — is a useful and legible core. A more complete ecology can additionally route fallen leaves, dead roots, dead plants, fruit remains, carcasses, and exudates through decomposers and mineralization before returning particular nutrients to accessible soil pools.

**Important separation:** Organic detritus is not instantly equivalent to plant-available mineral nutrient. Decomposition and mineralization can be represented as separate declared processes with rates influenced by moisture, temperature, substrate composition, and decomposer presence. This creates meaningful lag and prevents a same-tick infinite fertility loop.

### 3.7 Environmental sensing, tropisms, and plasticity

Plants respond to their surroundings through regulated growth. Relevant phenomena include:

- **Phototropism:** directional growth response to light; useful for modelling shoots bending toward useful illumination.
- **Gravitropism:** growth orientation response to gravity; useful for upward shoot orientation and downward root tendencies, with species- and organ-specific exceptions.
- **Thigmotropism and contact responses:** growth or movement triggered by contact, including climbing structures and tendril coiling.
- **Hydrotropic and nutrient-related responses:** changes in root growth in response to water and nutrient distributions.
- **Shade avoidance:** altered architecture in response to neighbouring vegetation and light quality.
- **Mechanical responses:** growth and reinforcement patterns affected by loading, wind, contact, and damage.

Phototropism research links directional light sensing to hormone-mediated differential growth ([phototropism review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3963583/)); broader reviews explain how light regulates growth, development, flowering, and shade avoidance ([light-mediated regulation review](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-arplant-043015-112252)). These mechanisms support **plasticity**: one genotype can develop different forms under different environments.

**Proposed extension:** Instead of selecting a single final mesh for each species, compute a developmental proposal based on local gradients and current organ state. The same oak profile might yield a fuller crown in abundant light, a one-sided crown beside a wall, a smaller crown under drought, or more root investment in a nutrient-poor patch. Each result should remain within species limits and the profile envelope.

### 3.8 Leaf arrangement and branching patterns

Leaf arrangement, or **phyllotaxis**, is a developmental pattern rather than random decoration. Common arrangements include alternate/spiral, opposite, and whorled patterns. In many spiral arrangements, successive organs are separated by an angle near 137.5 degrees, often called the golden angle; it is common but not a universal rule for all plant species ([Kuhlemeier, “Phyllotaxis”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17368962/)). Branching angles and the orientation of shoots also influence crown shape, spacing, and light capture ([2025 branching-angle review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590346225000549)).

**Generative implication:** Use a species-declared arrangement rule to place buds, leaves, branches, or flowers around a growth axis. Examples include fixed alternating angles, opposite pairs, whorls, or a golden-angle spiral. The rule should be deterministically generated from the organ's identity and developmental index; it should not depend on incidental iteration order.

A fixed golden angle can make a recognisable spiral but is not, by itself, a complete biology model. Organ spacing also depends on meristem geometry, organ size, developmental stage, and species traits. Use a species profile, not a global “Fibonacci plant” switch.

### 3.9 Mechanical support and size constraints

Plant structure must bear its own weight and environmental loads. Stem diameter, branch angle, taper, branching topology, tissue material properties, wind exposure, and water status all affect stability. Physical laws constrain plant shape as well as resource capture; scaling reviews connect transport, diffusion, elasticity, and other biophysical relations to plant growth and form ([Niklas & Hammond, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31070740/); [plant growth mechanics review](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article/70/14/3587/5498733)).

A voxel game can approximate this without finite-element simulation. For example:

- branches have minimum support thickness by age and expected load;
- unsupported overhangs have a declared maximum length;
- a branch's mass and attached canopy contribute to load;
- wind can bend flexible shoots or damage brittle ones;
- damaged or over-loaded branches can break via a declared process;
- trunk and root support scale with species traits and substrate conditions.

These mechanics should influence feasible growth, not secretly alter mass. A branch that cannot be supported can be rejected, shortened, reoriented, or later shed, each through a declared outcome.

### 3.10 Reproduction, dormancy, and dispersal

Plants can reproduce sexually through flowers, pollen, fruits, and seeds, or vegetatively through runners, rhizomes, bulbs, fragments, and other clonal structures. Seed establishment is a multi-stage process. Dormancy can delay germination until conditions become suitable; germination, emergence, establishment, and dispersal have different failure risks. A seed bank creates memory at the ecosystem level even when no adult plant is visible. Reviews treat dormancy, germination cues, seedling emergence, seed-bank persistence, reproduction, and dispersal as linked stages rather than independent random events ([seed dormancy review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1365-2435.14269); [seed-bank principles](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-24733-1); [2026 regeneration-strategies review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13341153/)).

**Proposed extension:** A species definition should declare reproduction modes, maturity requirements, propagule mass, dispersal modes, dormancy conditions, germination windows, establishment requirements, and reproductive costs. A seed is a matter-bearing entity or account, not a free spawn token. Its reserves are debited from the parent and transferred to the propagule; successful germination allocates some of that reserve to the new seedling, while failed seeds can remain dormant, decay, be consumed, or be transported through explicit processes.

### 3.11 Death, litter, damage, and succession

A complete life-cycle model needs more than “alive” and “removed.” Leaves can senesce; roots can die; wood can persist; an annual plant may complete its lifecycle; a perennial may enter dormancy; damage can suppress growth or induce repair; consumers can remove biomass; disease can change survival and reproduction; dead matter may decompose at rates different from living growth.

These processes are important for ecology and conservation because they return matter to the world and change the opportunity for future colonists. Gaia can respond to observable outcomes such as soil nutrient availability, species occupancy, carrying capacity, or measured harvest accounting. It should not infer sustainable practice solely from a player-applied label.

### 3.12 Competition and carrying capacity

Plants compete indirectly and directly for light, water, nutrients, rooting volume, space, pollinators, or dispersers. Competitive effects can depend on architecture: a tall crown shades neighbours, dense roots deplete a local resource, a vine occupies support surfaces, and a clonal plant expands laterally. **Carrying capacity** is therefore a declared gameplay approximation to a dynamic ecological constraint, not a universal fixed number supplied by biology.

The existing GAIA rule already requires a declared carrying capacity per region and a global entity ceiling. A more biologically expressive system can derive a region's available slots from admitted resource and habitat variables, while retaining an explicit hard cap. If the hard cap is reached, births are refused with a typed reason. LOD may compress distant populations only if the aggregate state conserves the data needed for later expansion and does not manufacture additional mass or births.

## 4. A layered architecture for Gaia and plants

### 4.1 Keep four models separate

A robust implementation should treat the following as distinct layers, joined only by declared interfaces.

| Layer | Responsibility | Must not do |
|---|---|---|
| **Biological state** | Organ graph, age/stage, reserves, stress, resource access, reproduction, damage | Decide policy hostility or grant actor authority |
| **Development and geometry** | Generate organs/segments and map them to cuboid shaper profiles and voxels | Create biomass or bypass growth preconditions |
| **Gaia disposition and policy** | Select among feasible ecological actions according to hostility, redemption, appreciation, claims, and active ruleset | Make an impossible biological action feasible or override conserved quantities |
| **Governance and ledger** | Validate authority boundaries, source accounts, atomicity, population limits, evidence, and declared process roster | Treat a preference as an accounting exception |

The separation makes it possible for a mod to rewrite Gaia's preference rules without having permission to rewrite conservation or authority invariants. It also allows species physiology and voxel shape profiles to evolve independently of the disposition model.

### 4.2 Suggested data model

These are proposed fields, not mandatory exact names. Equivalent project-native types should be preferred if they already exist.

**Species definition**

- `species_id` and schema/version;
- growth form: herb, grass, shrub, tree, liana/vine, fern-like, succulent, aquatic, parasitic, or another admitted form;
- life history: annual, biennial, perennial, clonal, or other declared type;
- developmental grammar or graph-construction rules;
- organ traits and allocation policy;
- environmental response functions and stress thresholds;
- reproductive and dispersal rules;
- species mass ceiling and maximum organ/architecture dimensions;
- allowed shaper profile identities by organ/stage;
- ecological interactions and danger class, if any;
- declared resource requirements and transfer coefficients.

**Plant instance**

- stable entity identity;
- species identity and inherited trait/profile version;
- position/rooting footprint and orientation;
- age, developmental stage, and dormancy state;
- organ graph with stable node identities;
- per-organ and total conserved matter accounts;
- stored reserves and stress indicators;
- local light, water, nutrient, competition, and damage observations;
- current reproduction/propagule state;
- current geometry cache, rebuildable from authoritative data;
- provenance and evidence links for matter-changing operations.

**Important:** Render caches, voxel occupancy, and derived crown bounds must be rebuildable. They are not the source of truth for biomass, species identity, authority, or historical evidence.

### 4.3 Deterministic growth cycle

A proposed process sequence for one plant or a bounded group is:

1. **Read authoritative state.** Read environmental variables, plant accounts, organ graph, region limits, and the active ruleset. Do not read wall-clock state or incidental UI state.
2. **Evaluate plant status.** Compute developmental stage, resource stress, viable buds, expected maintenance, reproductive readiness, and damage response.
3. **Generate candidate actions.** Examples: extend a stem, initiate a branch, expand a leaf, extend a root, produce a flower, set seed, grow a vine segment, enter dormancy, shed an organ.
4. **Apply biological feasibility.** Filter candidates through species rules, environmental thresholds, geometry support limits, water and nutrient constraints, and profile envelopes.
5. **Apply Gaia preference.** Select or weight among the feasible candidates using declared species/claim disposition and the active ruleset. Preferences may change behaviour, but may not invent capability.
6. **Reserve source accounts.** Calculate required matter and ensure every required source is available. Include carbon, water, minerals, propagule reserves, or other explicitly admitted accounts as relevant to the selected model.
7. **Resolve geometry and conflict.** Use stable priority and tie-break order for voxel occupancy, collisions, root overlap, canopy competition, actor movement, and region limits.
8. **Construct the delta.** Include all debits, credits, created/removed organ matter, and emitted/transferred matter represented by the model.
9. **Validate and commit atomically.** If any invariant fails, commit nothing; return a typed refusal naming the blocking condition. Do not partially grow a branch after its matter reservation failed.
10. **Record evidence and update derived state.** Record conserved delta, source accounts, process identity, region/cadence context, active Gaia ruleset identity, and a readable outcome. Rebuild or invalidate geometry caches as needed.

**Order-of-operations caution:** If resource competition is resolved by processing plants one at a time, iteration order can create hidden advantage. The scheduler should use stable job keys, declared conflict-resolution rules, or a bounded multi-phase reservation scheme. Never let a hash-map iteration order decide which plant receives the last available nutrient unit.

### 4.4 Determinism, replay, and mod changes

Within a fixed ruleset and fixed authoritative input state, the same process should produce the same result. Random-looking variation can be generated deterministically from stable identity-bound inputs, for example a hash of world seed, plant identity, organ identity, and growth event index. It must not depend on current wall-clock time, render frame rate, thread completion order, or container iteration order.

The source record explicitly makes a portability trade-off for Gaia ruleset identity: records name the active ruleset but do not identity-bind it, so saves remain portable across mod changes while exact reproduction across different rulesets is not guaranteed. Preserve that stated exception. A history viewer should say which ruleset produced the record; it should not claim the current ruleset will replay old behaviour identically.

## 5. Cuboid voxel shaper profiles for generative plant growth

### 5.1 What a shaper profile is

A **cuboid voxel shaper profile** is a declarative spatial envelope composed of axis-aligned boxes in an organ's local coordinate frame. When attached to a stem, root, branch, crown, leaf cluster, frond, or vine segment, the profile defines where that organ type is permitted or favoured to occupy voxels as it develops.

The central distinction is:

- A **plant-development rule** determines *what organ should be attempted and why*.
- A **shaper profile** determines *where that organ may fit and what spatial distribution is preferred*.
- The **mass/resource process** determines *whether the organ can actually be built*.
- The **voxel renderer** determines *how the accepted result appears in the world*.

A profile is not a full plant, and the profile's bounding volume is not biomass. Crown envelopes contain empty air; leaves and stems have different densities; roots can extend beyond the visible crown. A canopy profile that covers 400 voxels must not automatically debit the mass of 400 solid blocks.

### 5.2 Coordinate and box conventions

Use a consistent coordinate convention for all profiles. The examples below use:

- `x`: east/west horizontal axis;
- `y`: vertical axis, positive upward;
- `z`: north/south horizontal axis;
- integer coordinates measured in voxel units;
- half-open cuboid bounds: `min` is included and `max_exclusive` is excluded;
- local profile coordinates are transformed through the parent organ's stable transform before world voxelization.

For example, `min: [-2, 0, -1]`, `max_exclusive: [3, 4, 2]` covers five voxels in x, four in y, and three in z. Half-open bounds avoid off-by-one ambiguity when adjacent cuboids share a face.

A profile can use either:

1. **Explicit cuboids:** convenient for branch chunks, leaf clusters, root lobes, and asymmetric forms.
2. **Stacked slices:** a compact set of horizontal cuboids with widths that vary by height. This is effectively a stack of cuboids, easy to edit and audit.
3. **Weighted envelopes:** cuboids include density/priority fields that determine the probability or score for a candidate leaf or twig location. Weighted occupancy must use deterministic sampling or stable scoring.

An implementation may support all three, but should normalize them into a single internal profile representation before growth evaluation.

### 5.3 Proposed profile schema

The following YAML is a proposed human-readable convention. Exact field names can be adapted to the project's registry format.

```yaml
profile_schema: gaia.voxel_shaper.v1
profile_id: plant.tree.broadleaf.crown.v1
coordinate_frame: local_xyz_y_up
voxel_unit: world_voxel
purpose: canopy_envelope
bounds:
  min: [-5, 0, -4]
  max_exclusive: [6, 15, 5]
stage_range: [0.25, 1.0]       # normalized developmental stage, not real time
slices:
  - y: [0, 3]
    x_half_width: 1
    z_half_width: 1
    occupancy_weight: 0.20
    organ_roles: [twig, sparse_leaf]
  - y: [3, 6]
    x_half_width: 2
    z_half_width: 2
    occupancy_weight: 0.42
    organ_roles: [branch, leaf]
  - y: [6, 10]
    x_half_width: 4
    z_half_width: 3
    occupancy_weight: 0.68
    organ_roles: [branch, leaf]
  - y: [10, 13]
    x_half_width: 3
    z_half_width: 3
    occupancy_weight: 0.55
    organ_roles: [twig, leaf, flower]
  - y: [13, 15]
    x_half_width: 1
    z_half_width: 1
    occupancy_weight: 0.25
    organ_roles: [tip, sparse_leaf]
constraints:
  max_branch_order: 5
  allow_growth_outside_envelope: false
  leaf_placement: species_phyllotaxis
  deterministic_candidate_order: stable_path_key
mass_model:
  voxel_occupancy_is_mass: false
  organ_mass_account: required
```

`occupancy_weight` is a geometric preference, not a promise that every voxel will be filled. A profile may be clipped by terrain, collision, light competition, mass ceiling, or resource shortage. If `allow_growth_outside_envelope` is enabled for a root or wandering vine, that exception should be explicit and bounded.

### 5.4 How profiles participate in generative growth

A robust pipeline should not fill every voxel in a cuboid and call the result a plant. Instead:

1. **Build or extend the organ graph.** A grammar or developmental rule proposes an organ from a parent growth point.
2. **Choose an organ-specific profile.** A stem, trunk, branch, leaf, root, frond, and vine segment should not all use the same envelope.
3. **Transform the profile.** Map local coordinates into the organ's local orientation and then to world space. For rotated branches, do the geometry calculation in a stable fixed-point or explicitly quantized transform so replay does not depend on floating-point quirks across platforms.
4. **Generate candidate placements.** Candidates may be voxel centres, organ endpoints, leaf anchors, or branch segments. Generate them in stable order.
5. **Score candidates.** Use profile occupancy weight, light exposure, collision status, support, moisture/nutrient access, species preference, and developmental stage.
6. **Apply deterministic selection.** A threshold or ranked selection can use stable identity-bound hashes; avoid consuming a shared global random stream whose results shift when unrelated plants are added.
7. **Check matter and constraints.** Accepted organs need mass, water, and nutrient transfers under the declared plant-growth process. A geometric candidate is not proof that the plant can afford it.
8. **Commit or refuse.** The accepted organ and its matter delta are committed atomically. A refused growth action must leave both geometry and accounts unchanged.
9. **Rebuild the rendered occupancy.** Voxel geometry is a derived representation of the accepted organ graph and profiles.

### 5.5 Growth-stage scaling

A shaper should support plant development without requiring a unique hand-authored profile for every age. One approach is to define a normalized stage value `s` in `[0, 1]`, then scale the profile's local bounding dimensions using a declared, species-specific function.

A simple linear scale can be useful for early prototypes:

`scale(s) = s`

But biological growth is not generally linear over a whole life cycle. A better implementation can declare separate curves for height, crown width, root reach, stem diameter, and reproductive output. A generic saturating curve such as

`scale(s) = s / (s + k * (1 - s))`

can offer a flexible shape, but the parameter `k` is an engineering control, not a universal biological constant. A logistic, piecewise, allometric, or measured-species curve may be more appropriate. The growth function should be stored in admitted species data, not hard-coded into the renderer.

The envelope's geometric scale does not determine biomass. Matter costs should be calculated from the organs the developmental model actually proposes and accepts.

### 5.6 Example A — broadleaf tree crown and tapering trunk

**Use:** An oak-like or maple-like broad crown with an upright trunk. This is a shape abstraction, not a claim that all broadleaf trees have the same crown geometry.

```yaml
    organ_roles: [trunk]

```

Pair the trunk profile with a crown envelope such as the schema in §5.3 and an organ grammar that creates lateral branches at declared heights and angles. A broadleaf grammar might use several branch orders, non-uniform branch lengths, and a species-specific leaf arrangement. Local light availability can shift the probability of branch initiation toward open space while preserving maximum branch order and profile bounds.

**Expected emergent outcomes:**

- isolated trees tend to form more balanced crowns;
- trees beside obstacles can form an asymmetric crown if light-response rules bias growth toward open directions;
- a young tree occupies only a scaled portion of the crown envelope;
- drought or nutrient limitation can result in fewer branches/leaves because the growth process cannot pay for them;
- a broken leader may activate a declared lateral replacement rule rather than regenerating a perfect original mesh.

### 5.14 Irregularity without nondeterminism

Natural plants are not perfectly symmetrical. Introduce irregularity through declared rules rather than uncontrolled randomness:

- deterministic per-branch length variation keyed by branch identity;
- bounded angle changes sampled from a stable hash;
- directional bias derived from light or obstacle gradients;
- branch loss generated by explicit damage, disease, or support failure;
- age-dependent asymmetry from branch survival and competition;
- profile variants selected from declared species or life-stage data.

Avoid a shared random stream. If adding one unrelated plant changes the random numbers consumed by every existing tree, then saving/loading or changing job scheduling can alter old plants. Stable identity-bound variation is both more reproducible and easier to audit.

### 5.15 Voxel occupancy, organ mass, and biological volume

Use separate variables for:

1. **Geometry:** which voxels overlap an organ or canopy envelope;
2. **Material volume:** an estimate of the actual solid/tissue volume represented by the organ;
3. **Mass:** the conserved quantity used for matter accounting and GAIA's threat threshold;
4. **Visual density:** how full an envelope appears to a renderer;
5. **Physiology:** photosynthetic area, storage capacity, water transport, or other functional traits.

They may be related by declared functions, but they are not synonyms. A leaf canopy is largely air; a trunk voxel may represent a large solid volume; root voxels may stand for many fine roots; a plant rendered at lower detail may have fewer voxels but the same authoritative mass. Any conversion used by combat, carrying capacity, harvesting, or conservation must operate on an authoritative conserved quantity, never on a presentation-only voxel count.

## 6. Linking shapers to botanical architecture models

### 6.1 L-systems and graph grammars

L-systems rewrite symbols or structures according to production rules and can generate branching plant forms. They have been used to model plant development at different scales, including descriptive and more mechanistic approaches ([Prusinkiewicz, Cieslak, Ferraro & Hanan](https://algorithmicbotany.org/papers/modeling-plant-development-with-l-systems.html)). Graph grammars or explicit organ graphs offer a similar way to represent parent-child relationships while making identity, age, and resource allocation easier to retain in a stateful simulation.

A schematic rule set might be:

```text
Apex -> StemSegment + (BranchBud at declared node) + Apex
BranchBud -> BranchSegment + LeafBud + BranchBud
LeafBud -> LeafOrgan
MatureApex -> FlowerBud        when stage and resource conditions pass
FlowerBud -> Fruit/Seed        when pollination and development conditions pass
```

This is an illustration only. The actual grammar must define which branches are optional, what stops an apex, how organ identities are generated, what resource costs apply, and which cues alter a production rule.

### 6.2 Functional–structural coupling

Functional–structural plant models couple the 3D structure of a plant to processes that determine how it acquires and allocates resources. A recent platform-agnostic guide lays out four core elements—function, geometry, topology, and environment—and explicitly discusses organ meshes, voxels, crown envelopes, L-systems, and graph grammars as alternative representations ([2026 FSPM guide](https://academic.oup.com/insilicoplants/article/8/2/diag023/8770950)).

The recommended GAIA mapping is:

| FSPM concept | GAIA representation |
|---|---|
| Function | photosynthetic proxy, water/nutrient acquisition, allocation, stress, reproduction, maintenance |
| Geometry | cuboid voxel shaper profiles, organ segments, bounds, voxel occupancy cache |
| Topology | stable organ graph, parent-child links, branching order, vine actor path |
| Environment | authoritative light, temperature, moisture, soil accounts, obstruction, wind, neighbour occupancy |
| Development | declared growth processes, graph grammar, stage transitions, organ initiation |
| Verification | conserved deltas, atomic validation, stable keys, evidence records, typed refusals |

This division enables multiple fidelity levels. A distant tree can use a crown envelope and aggregate physiology; a nearby tree can use explicit branches and leaf clusters. Both levels must reconcile to the same authoritative state.

### 6.3 Crown envelopes as a cheaper representation

For far-field trees, a crown envelope can approximate how much light a plant captures or how much space it occupies without simulating every leaf voxel. A profile could record crown height, horizontal radii, opacity/light-transmission proxy, and organ-mass totals. When the player approaches, the system can refine the representation into explicit branches, provided the refined model derives from the aggregate state rather than inventing extra biomass.

### 6.4 Multiscale representation and LOD

A useful hierarchy is:

- **LOD 0 — organ detail:** explicit branches, leaves, roots, fruits, and per-organ updates near the player;
- **LOD 1 — structural detail:** trunks, primary branches, crown slices, coarse root zones, aggregate leaves;
- **LOD 2 — population aggregate:** species count by age/stage and region, biomass pools, seed bank, resource demand, occupancy envelopes;
- **LOD 3 — very distant biome state:** bounded species/resource summaries adequate to preserve large-scale succession and future simulation.

A lower level of detail is not a lesser conservation standard. It can reduce geometry and per-instance processing, but aggregate matter, reproduction, mortality, resource uptake, and capacity effects must remain auditable. Conversion between LOD tiers must be a declared, deterministic transformation; no “expand to full trees” event may create matter or population that was not held in the aggregate record.

## 7. Gaia's disposition model alongside plant physiology

### 7.1 Two decision questions, not one

The biological model asks: **What can this plant do under its current physiology, environment, architecture, and resources?** Gaia asks: **Among feasible ecological actions, which does Gaia prefer to initiate or favour in this species/claim context?**

The two questions must be evaluated separately. If a hostile plant lacks enough mass, its hostility cannot make it pass the defined threat comparison. If a species has exhausted its growth resources, appreciation cannot mint carbon. If a vine's path is blocked and no permitted path exists, disposition cannot invent a traversable route. Conversely, a benign disposition is not a guarantee that a plant will survive drought or avoid natural competition.

### 7.2 Disposition changes behaviour, not physical law

Examples of safe coupling:

- a highly appreciated plant species may receive higher priority among otherwise feasible growth candidates;
- a hostile species may expand toward an unprotected claim if its admitted biology and mass resources permit it;
- a redeemable species may reduce aggressive behaviour after a visible qualifying act and declared period;
- an appreciated colony may receive more tolerance for measured sustainable practices;
- Gaia may direct mobile flora around a declared barrier rather than attempting to change the barrier's matter state.

Examples of unsafe coupling:

- setting a plant's mass to a number that crosses a citizen's threshold;
- filling a crown's profile without source-account transfers;
- allowing a vine to bite because a shape resembles a mouth;
- converting a refusal into a silent partial growth;
- applying Gaia disposition directly to soil fertility or terrain without a declared process;
- treating a practice tag as proof that its promised ecological outcome occurred.

### 7.3 Species and claim aggregation

The source design stores disposition by species and claim, not every plant. That is both a cost control and a policy choice. Biological variation can still exist per individual—size, age, damage, genotype, local water access—without requiring an individual Gaia disposition score. If a mod needs an individual exception such as “protect this particular oak,” represent it as a declared claim or a mod-level per-individual claim, as the source permits, rather than quietly changing the default aggregation model.

### 7.4 Visible outcomes and honest instrumentation

Because disposition is hidden, the interface should reveal evidence such as:

- the number of observed growth attempts and their outcomes, at an appropriate aggregation level;
- visible path changes or spatial avoidance;
- measured soil or plant population changes;
- typed refusals that are appropriate to disclose;
- the active ruleset identity in the audit view;
- whether a metric is measured, estimated, or merely a design target.

It should not display a made-up “Gaia likes you by 78%” indicator before the technology that authorizes reading disposition. If a displayed metric is an estimate derived from aggregate state, label it as an estimate.

## 8. Ecological processes and candidate registry vocabulary

The source record leaves the practice vocabulary (`0154`) open. The following is a **candidate taxonomy** for later registry admission. It is not a list of already approved processes.

### 8.1 Plant lifecycle processes

| Process candidate | Inputs and preconditions | Outputs/effects | Required safeguards |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germinate propagule | viable seed/spore, permitted moisture/temperature/light cues, reserve mass | seedling organ graph and transferred reserves | reserve balance, germination state, carrying capacity |
| Extend organ | viable growth point, source accounts, profile space, stage allows growth | new organ and conserved matter delta | mass/nutrient/water check, profile bounds, stable identity |
| Uptake water | accessible root reach, available soil water, hydraulic capacity | water transferred into plant account | no duplicate withdrawal; soil and plant balances |
| Acquire mineral nutrient | accessible root reach and available nutrient form | nutrient transferred into plant | nutrient-specific balance; no negative soil account |
| Assimilate carbon | light and CO₂ availability, photosynthetic organ state | carbon stored or allocated | light/CO₂/energy proxy; explicit carbon source account |
| Allocate reserves | stored matter, declared source–sink priority | matter transferred to a destination organ | transfer conservation; destination capacity |
| Flower/fruit/seed | maturity and resource conditions; pollination if required | reproductive organs and propagules | reproductive cost; population cap; seed matter ledger |
| Disperse propagule | mature propagule, declared dispersal mechanism | propagule position/transport state | identity persistence, bounded movement, no free spawn |
| Senesce/shedding | age, season, stress, damage, life-history rule | dead/shed organ, detritus pool | conserved transfer, no disappearance of mass |
| Decompose litter | detritus, decomposer activity, moisture/temperature | mineral nutrients, gases, residual organic matter | declared conversion and element balance at chosen fidelity |
| Herbivory or plant damage | admitted actor capability, contact/range, target conditions | biomass transfer, wound state, possible death | authority, target identity, conservation |
| Clonal spread | available parent reserve and eligible site | connected ramet or new vegetative unit | parent debit, space/capacity, unique identity |
| Enter dormancy | species-specific cue and viable reserve state | dormant state with altered process schedule | no hidden time passage; retained accounts |
| Recover from dormancy | declared release conditions | renewed growth scheduling | reserve and environmental checks |

### 8.2 Candidate sustainable-practice tags

A sustainable-practice tag should identify an **intent category** that makes specific metrics relevant. It does not prove success. Possible candidates include:

- `soil_fertility_maintained` — examine trends in accessible nutrient pools and soil organic matter proxies;
- `water_withdrawal_bounded` — compare uptake with declared replenishment and water-account floors;
- `harvest_mass_accounted` — require all harvested matter to be sourced from a declared plant or storage account;
- `habitat_capacity_preserved` — test whether the practice drives a species' admitted carrying capacity to zero;
- `propagule_source_preserved` — examine whether harvest removes all local reproductive capacity;
- `native_or_target_species_retained` — compare observed populations against the declared target set;
- `soil_cover_retained` — measure a declared cover proxy, not infer cover from intent;
- `decomposition_path_provided` — verify that removed plant matter enters a declared detritus or disposal account.

**Measured-outcome discipline:** A tag may say what the player intended; the metric says what happened. If the metric is unavailable, Gaia should not claim success. If the outcome is mixed, the system should preserve the actual values and apply the declared evaluation function rather than converting the result into a binary moral verdict.

### 8.3 Candidate biological state variables

Avoid accumulating many opaque “ecology scores.” Start with interpretable quantities tied to specific processes:

- available water by soil region and depth band;
- mineral nutrient pools by element or declared nutrient class;
- organic detritus and decomposer activity proxy;
- light availability by voxel or canopy layer;
- temperature/seasonal cue and frost/heat stress;
- plant dry-matter or elemental accounts, plus water stores if represented;
- leaf area or photosynthetic-area proxy;
- root reach and uptake capacity;
- age/stage, dormancy, reproductive status, damage, and mortality risk;
- region occupancy, species density, seed bank, and carrying capacity.

A metric should have a unit, update process, source, and known limitations. If the game only models a rough proxy, name it as a proxy.

## 9. Plant movement and deadly flora boundaries

### 9.1 Rooted growth versus mobile actors

Most plants should remain rooted organisms whose geometry expands via the growth process. Vines with the special mobile behaviour defined by GAIA are different: the vine is an actor with a path and movement delta. It may subsequently generate a stem shape from its path. This distinction must remain visible in the data model:

- rooted plant: position anchors the root system; growth adds organs;
- clonal plant: parent-linked ramets extend through a declared spread process;
- mobile vine: actor position/target/path state changes under the governed actor pipeline, and body geometry follows that state;
- ordinary climbing vine: rooted base with growing stem that attaches to supports; no autonomous actor movement unless specifically declared.

Do not make all vines mobile simply because the renderer represents them as a sequence of voxels.

### 9.2 Attack capability is not implied by species shape

A plant is dangerous only if its species capability and the governing rules admit the behaviour. For deadly plants, the source rule requires that the plant exceed a declared mass fraction of the citizen's conserved mass and remain under its own species ceiling. This threshold should be evaluated using authoritative mass. If the plant attacks, its attack action is a declared actor interaction with a target, range, cadence, and damage transfer—not a voxel collision side effect.

### 9.3 Damage and defence

Defensive traits might include irritant contact, thorns, toxins, concealment, rapid regrowth, or structural resistance. These should be explicit capabilities with admitted triggers and costs. A visual thorn profile alone must not apply damage. A poison effect should use a declared effect and target rule. If defensive chemicals consume biomass or nutrients in the simulation, their production has a matter cost. A simplified game may instead treat a chemical trait as a bounded capability parameter, but then the abstraction should be documented rather than presented as a literal chemical inventory.

## 10. Soil, detritus, and ecosystem memory

### 10.1 Recommended matter-flow diagram

A fuller ecological loop can be represented as:

```text
Atmospheric CO₂ + light energy + water + mineral nutrients
                    │
                    ▼
        plant assimilation and growth
                    │
          ┌─────────┼──────────┐
          ▼         ▼          ▼
       living      seeds      exudates
       organs       and        │
          │       fruits       ▼
          │                    soil biota
          ▼                    │
   senescence / death           │
          │                    │
          └─────────┬──────────┘
                    ▼
             detritus pool
                    │
        decomposition / mineralization
                    │
                    ▼
          soil nutrient accounts
                    │
                    └──────► plant uptake
```

The diagram is a systems sketch, not a chemical reaction equation. Respiration, gas exchange, herbivory, waste, leaching, erosion, fire, and other processes can be added as admitted transfers. Each arrow that changes an authoritative matter account must map to a declared process and evidence record.

### 10.2 Do not collapse all soil qualities into one number

A single fertility number is understandable for simple gameplay, but it hides important interactions. A richer model can separate:

- water availability;
- nitrogen-like nutrient availability;
- phosphorus-like nutrient availability;
- exchangeable mineral pool proxy;
- organic matter/detritus;
- pH/salinity or other stress proxies;
- soil depth/penetrability;
- microbiome or decomposer activity proxy.

This does not mean every world must simulate all variables. It means that when a variable affects a process, its meaning and source should be explicit. A plant's nutrient uptake should not debit an abstract fertility score and a separate nitrogen account for the same nutrient unless the relationship is formally defined.

### 10.3 Spatial locality and delayed feedback

Root uptake and decomposition are local; nutrients do not instantly become available everywhere. A useful grid-level model allows detritus to accumulate in one cell, decomposers to process it over multiple ticks, and the released nutrient to become available to roots that reach that cell. This spatial delay enables ecological niches and makes plant architecture consequential. It also keeps the mass ledger legible: where the matter is, who owns it, and which process transfers it are separate questions.

## 11. Deterministic voxel growth: implementation contract

### 11.1 Stable identifiers

Generate organ identifiers from stable parent identity and developmental indices, not current list positions. A conceptual form is:

`organ_id = stable_id(plant_id, parent_organ_id, production_rule_id, initiation_index)`

The exact construction must follow the project's identity requirements. It should not change when an unrelated leaf or plant is inserted elsewhere.

### 11.2 Stable candidate sorting

Before accepting voxel or organ candidates, sort by a declared key, for example:

`(plant_id, organ_id, candidate_y, candidate_z, candidate_x, candidate_role)`

Choose an ordering appropriate to the actual data model and declare it. Do not rely on dictionary iteration or thread race completion. If priority is needed, incorporate a declared priority field and a final deterministic key as a tie-break.

### 11.3 Fixed-point and quantization

If cross-platform replay matters, use fixed-point arithmetic or specify quantization points for rotations, light scores, resource thresholds, and profile transforms. Integer voxel bounds alone do not guarantee deterministic geometry if floating-point transforms vary. Document the chosen precision and rounding rule.

### 11.4 Atomic growth delta

An accepted organ growth event should validate as a unit. It may contain several coordinated transfers—for example carbon, water, minerals, and stored reserves—plus the new organ state. If one required debit fails, the whole event should refuse. Do not consume a small amount of reserve and then leave an incomplete stem segment unless partial growth is itself a declared process with its own valid state.

### 11.5 Typed refusals

Refusals should identify the actual blocker, such as:

- `insufficient_atmospheric_carbon_account`;
- `insufficient_water_account`;
- `insufficient_mineral_nutrient`;
- `species_mass_ceiling_reached`;
- `regional_carrying_capacity_reached`;
- `global_entity_ceiling_reached`;
- `profile_envelope_exceeded`;
- `unsupported_branch_geometry`;
- `occupied_voxel_conflict`;
- `blocked_vine_path`;
- `growth_stage_not_eligible`;
- `source_account_mismatch`;
- `authority_or_process_not_admitted`.

These are proposed labels. The canonical project registry should own the final enum. A refusal must not be turned into a silent drop or an unexplained “nothing happened.”

### 11.6 Evidence record contents

At a minimum, a mass-bearing growth record should allow an auditor to reconstruct:

- process identity and version;
- plant/entity identity and species identity;
- active GAIA ruleset identity where the action is Gaia-driven;
- world/region/cadence/tick reference;
- source accounts and debits;
- destination accounts and credits;
- organ additions/removals or other state delta;
- profile identity/version and developmental rule identity;
- resource and geometry checks that governed acceptance;
- commit/refusal result and typed reason;
- deterministic ordering key or enough information to establish it;
- links to the relevant before/after authoritative state or audit delta.

Per-source rules, every mass-bearing Gaia action requires a conserved delta and full evidence. This per-action growth evidence is not a per-entity Gaia-disposition decision log; those remain distinct concepts.

## 12. Validation plan and invariants

The model should be tested with invariant checks, controlled scenarios, and replay tests. A visually plausible tree is not sufficient evidence that the ecological system works.

### 12.1 Conservation tests

1. **No-growth identity:** A plant with zero admitted source resources cannot grow new matter.
2. **Carbon-source accounting:** New organic matter cannot appear without debiting the declared carbon source or an existing reserve account.
3. **Seed transfer:** Creating a seed debits the parent or reproductive account; germination transfers reserves rather than minting a free seedling.
4. **Leaf fall:** A shed leaf leaves the living-organ account and enters a declared detritus, consumption, or disposal account.
5. **Root uptake:** Soil nutrient debit equals plant nutrient credit at the chosen accounting precision.
6. **Failed atomic growth:** If any required debit fails, neither the new organ nor any partial account change remains committed.
7. **LOD conservation:** Moving a population from detailed plants to an aggregate and back preserves total mass and the declared reproductive/age information.
8. **Voxel independence:** Changing renderer resolution or profile voxelization must not alter authoritative mass unless a declared process explicitly changes the physical model.

### 12.2 Biological plausibility tests

1. **Light asymmetry:** An obstructed side of a light-responsive plant can develop a different crown shape from the open side.
2. **Water limitation:** Low root-accessible water reduces growth according to declared thresholds; it does not necessarily reduce every plant process identically.
3. **Root foraging:** Roots preferentially extend into accessible, beneficial resource gradients only where the profile and matter budget permit it.
4. **Seasonal dormancy:** A species with an admitted seasonal dormant stage stops or changes the relevant developmental processes until a release condition is satisfied.
5. **Reproductive cost:** Flowering and seed production consume declared resources and may trade off against vegetative growth.
6. **Mechanical support:** Unsupported or over-loaded branches are rejected, shortened, reoriented, damaged, or shed through an admitted rule.
7. **Species variation:** Trees, grasses, shrubs, vines, ferns, and succulents use different architecture and response traits, not one universal branching rule.
8. **No false universals:** A golden-angle leaf arrangement is used only by species whose profile declares that pattern; it is not applied to all plants.

### 12.3 Gaia/governance tests

1. **Advisory refusal:** Refusing Gaia's request does not physically erase or prevent all future activity; later activity must remain visible and governed.
2. **Binding intervention:** Gaia cannot undo a player's matter-level fence or other physical intervention outside the declared process roster.
3. **No substrate bypass:** Plant roots or Gaia cannot directly mutate terrain unless a terrain-process entry allows it.
4. **Disposition independence:** A change in hostility/appreciation cannot modify conserved mass or source-account values directly.
5. **Mass threshold:** Deadly plant capability uses authoritative mass and the declared citizen-mass fraction, plus the species ceiling.
6. **Vine capability:** A vine cannot bite unless the canonical capability system explicitly permits it; the source GAIA rule says vines cannot bite.
7. **Capacity refusal:** Births over the regional or global limit produce typed refusals; no silent spawn is permitted.
8. **Ruleset traceability:** Gaia records name the active ruleset. Replay under a different ruleset is not falsely presented as identical.
9. **Hidden disposition:** The ordinary UI does not expose disposition numbers before a technology unlock permits it.
10. **Practice measurement:** A sustainability tag alone never confirms a positive outcome.

### 12.4 Determinism tests

- Replay the same authoritative snapshot twice under the same ruleset and compare the full state delta.
- Change thread scheduling and verify identical accepted organ candidates and resource winners.
- Insert an unrelated plant and verify existing plant IDs, variation keys, and growth outcomes do not shift merely because a shared random stream advanced.
- Save and load between candidate generation and commit; the resumed action should commit the same delta or refuse with the same declared reason.
- Run profile transforms on the supported platforms and compare quantized candidate voxels.
- Deliberately change the active Gaia ruleset and confirm the ledger records the changed identity without claiming cross-ruleset replay identity.

## 13. Performance strategy

### 13.1 Different cadences for different costs

The source design intentionally gives Gaia its own cadence separate from the generic ecology tick. Preserve the distinction. Plant physiology can update at one declared cadence; expensive Gaia disposition assessments can run less frequently or adapt based on authoritative regional/claim state; actor movement may have its own bounded tick. All cadence intervals must be derived from declared functions and authoritative state, not wall-clock timing or frame rate.

### 13.2 Work budgets and deterministic truncation

A bounded system needs explicit work limits: maximum candidate organs per plant per tick, maximum pathfinding expansions, maximum region jobs, maximum profile-resolution work, and maximum population expansion. If a budget is exhausted, the system should return a typed deferred/refused result or schedule a deterministic continuation. It should not randomly drop whichever branch happened to run last.

### 13.3 Spatial caches are derived state

Cache candidate voxels, canopy envelopes, nearby light blockers, accessible soil cells, and collision queries as needed. A cache must be invalidated when its authoritative dependencies change. It must not become an alternate authority source for mass, terrain, or actor permissions. The source of truth remains the organ graph, terrain/process state, accounts, and committed deltas.

### 13.4 Use coarse profiles where detail has low value

An individual leaf voxel may matter near the player, for harvesting, or for a detailed screenshot; it may matter very little for a forest several kilometres away. Use canopy envelopes and aggregate state at distance. Expand to organs only when needed, driven by deterministic rules and a known aggregate state. Keep the transition auditable and avoid any representation-specific mass changes.

## 14. Modding, profiles, and compatibility

### 14.1 Profile versions are data identities

A plant's profile can change when a mod changes. The ledger should retain the profile identity/version used for any committed action whose geometry or material cost depends on that profile. This does not require the profile to be identity-bound in every core context; it requires enough provenance to explain historical geometry and deltas.

### 14.2 Separate mod extension points from protected invariants

A privileged mod can alter preference rules, hostility, branching traits, species, or profile assets through admitted interfaces. It must not be able to make a mass-bearing action bypass atomic transfers, invalidate actor authority, exceed the canonical global ceiling, or mutate terrain outside the declared process roster.

### 14.3 Compatibility behavior

On loading a save with a missing profile version, the system should not silently replace historical matter or respawn the plant. Possible declared options include:

- use a bundled compatibility profile solely for rendering old geometry;
- retain the authoritative organ graph and mark geometry as partially unresolved;
- migrate the profile through an explicit versioned transform whose effect is recorded;
- refuse a destructive migration and report the missing dependency.

A profile is presentation/development data, but when it changes which organ candidates are accepted, it participates in world behaviour and must be treated as such.

## 15. Open decisions and recommended order of work

The source design identifies three open areas: the admitted sustainable-practice vocabulary, the Gaia technology branch, and the interaction between modifiable Gaia rules and non-identity-bound ruleset records. The scientific and voxel expansion introduces related questions. Recommended order:

### Priority 1 — Formalize conservation inputs for plant growth

Specify whether the matter ledger tracks total biomass only or elemental accounts. If biomass is coarse-grained, define how CO₂, water, mineral nutrients, reserves, respiration, litter, and gas exchange map into the conserved representation. Do not ship a biologically framed system whose plant mass silently appears from soil fertility alone.

### Priority 2 — Approve a minimal plant process roster

Start with seed/propagule creation, germination, organ growth, water uptake, nutrient uptake, carbon assimilation proxy, reserve allocation, reproduction, senescence, detritus transfer, and decomposition. Add more detailed chemistry or symbiosis only when it produces visible or measurable gameplay value.

### Priority 3 — Establish the shaper schema and conformance tests

Admit the canonical cuboid profile schema, coordinate conventions, half-open bounds, stable candidate order, weight semantics, stage scaling, profile versioning, and the rule that geometry is not mass. Include at least the broadleaf, conifer, shrub, grass, fern, vine, root, and succulent examples as test fixtures or equivalent fixtures.

### Priority 4 — Implement the organ graph and FSPM loop

Ensure architecture and physiology share a state model. Avoid having a visual generator build a tree first and then invent physiology after the fact. A growth candidate should be assessed for both biological feasibility and account availability before commit.

### Priority 5 — Finish population and LOD accounting

Define how counts, biomass, seed banks, developmental stages, mortality, and carrying capacity survive representation changes. The global entity ceiling remains a hard limit; LOD is a performance strategy rather than a hidden population loophole.

### Priority 6 — Admit sustainable-practice tags and outcome functions

Keep intent tags separate from measurements. Start with a small vocabulary and explicit metrics. Ensure the UI shows observed evidence and uncertainty rather than assigning unsupported success labels.

### Priority 7 — Design Gaia technology nodes

Define the capabilities that progressively permit reading disposition, requesting instead of merely acting, and opening redemption paths. Preserve the source's intended discovery arc: early play teaches players to infer from outcomes; later technology reveals a previously hidden internal state.

### Priority 8 — Document mod/ruleset history

Keep active ruleset identity in Gaia decision records. Build tooling that explains which ruleset and profile version were active without pretending that a changed ruleset will reproduce historical decisions exactly.

## 16. Recommended baseline specification

For a first implementation that is both practical and biologically meaningful, the baseline should include:

1. **Organ graph:** stems, branches, leaves, roots, reproductive organs, and propagules with stable identities.
2. **Species traits:** growth form, stage, branching and leaf arrangement, resource thresholds, mass ceiling, reproduction, dispersal, stress, and profile selection.
3. **Three explicit primary constraints:** light, root-accessible water, and mineral nutrient availability, plus carbon source accounting.
4. **Conserved process pipeline:** candidate → validate → reserve → atomic commit/refusal → evidence.
5. **Cuboid shaper profiles:** weighted bounds by organ and developmental stage, with a clear separation of geometry and mass.
6. **Environmental plasticity:** directional growth, resource-driven allocation, and bounded damage/recovery responses.
7. **Life cycle:** germination, growth, maturity, reproduction, dormancy where applicable, senescence, death, and detritus transfer.
8. **Gaia disposition:** hostility, redemption, and appreciation per species/claim, applied only after biological feasibility is established.
9. **Population controls:** regional carrying capacity, global entity ceiling, typed refusals, and conserved LOD.
10. **Evidence:** auditable deltas, stable keys, active ruleset identity, profile/process identities, and honest UI claims.

The baseline intentionally does not require cellular simulation, detailed hormone transport, accurate xylem hydraulics, full microbiome dynamics, or finite-element mechanics. Those are later fidelity layers. The first milestone should prove that the state model can produce diverse, resource-limited, persistent growth without breaking conservation, determinism, authority, or the game's performance budget.

## 17. Conclusion

Gaia's central architectural idea remains intact: ecological life can act autonomously without being exempted from conservation, atomicity, evidence, deterministic processes, bounded populations, or the player's ability to make binding physical interventions. Scientific plant modelling strengthens this design by replacing static plant shapes with a connection between developmental architecture, environmental signals, material resources, and life-cycle transitions.

Cuboid voxel shaper profiles provide a practical geometric vocabulary for this system. They make broad crowns, tapered conifers, shrubs, grasses, ferns, succulents, roots, and mobile-vine bodies editable as data. Their correct role is to constrain and bias organ placement—not to fill a volume indiscriminately, not to serve as biomass accounting, and not to take over the actor or governance model.

The most important technical boundary is the connection between biology and the ledger. Atmospheric carbon, water, mineral nutrients, stored reserves, growth, shedding, decomposition, and reproduction should pass through explicitly declared accounts and processes. Gaia's preferences then influence which feasible process is selected, while the evidence record explains what happened. That division lets a fictional ecology agent feel purposeful and unpredictable at the player level while remaining auditable and scientifically informed at the systems level.
